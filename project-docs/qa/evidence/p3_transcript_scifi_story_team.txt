# THE ATTRITION LIGHT

## Final Polished Version

---

The sea had taken everything worth taking, and now it demanded the small things that remained.

Aldous Vane climbed the tower at dusk, as he had climbed it every dusk for three years, and the iron rang under his boots like a bell abandoned in a graveyard. One hundred and forty steps. He counted them not because he could forget the number, but because counting was the last tether he had—some fraying cord between himself and the nothing that pressed in from every direction. The fog rolled in off the water, thick as old blood; it pressed against the glass of the lamp room like a face, suffocating and without expression. He had stopped imagining whose face it might be. That was how you survived out here. You stopped imagining.

He lit the beacon at 6:14, precisely. Precision was the last dignity the expendable were permitted. The lamp shuddered to life with a low sound—not mechanical, not quite—something between a sigh and the death rattle of an exhausted animal. The beam swept out over the drowned world: a flat expanse of brown water that had once been a city of nine million people. Somewhere beneath that water was a street he had lived on. Somewhere beneath that brown surface was a small house with a door he could no longer remember the color of. Red, he told himself. It was red. He reached for the certainty and found only wet sand. He scribbled in the logbook: *Door was red.* The words looked hollow even as the ink dried, and he crossed them out in a single hard stroke.

"Evening, Aldous." The comms crackled, and the woman's voice drifted through—warm as decay. Wren, she called herself. From the relay station inland, though he had never seen it, and lately he had begun to doubt it existed at all. "Nominal night on your end?"

"Nominal," he said, though the word tasted like something left too long in the dark. He hesitated. "The beam's doing that thing again."

"What thing?"

"Bending. Downward. It still sweeps the horizon, but it dips—like it's searching for something beneath the surface instead of warning ships off." He laughed, the sound cracking at its edges, something brittle that couldn't quite connect to the man producing it. "Not that there are ships. Not that there are rocks I haven't already memorized by grief."

A silence filled the line. In it, he heard something—a vast, patient sound, the slow respiration of the sea itself. Then Wren, gentle as a sedative: "Lens calibration drifts in the damp. Don't go up into the housing to fix it—that's not in your contract. Just keep the light on. That's all we ever needed you to do. Keep the light on and keep yourself company."

*Keep yourself company.* The phrase twisted in his mind long after the channel went silent, curling into something that no longer resembled kindness. It was the grammar of a cage. A door left open just enough to suggest freedom while the walls held.

---

Mara returned on the ninth night of the fog.

He knew she wasn't real—or at least he believed he knew this, which was not the same thing. He was a rational man. A hydrology engineer: all numbers, all models, all forecast overlaid with despair. He had trusted equations over hope and been proven right in the most devastating way possible. Grief manufactured ghosts; the mind would cannibalize whatever it had left to build itself company. So when he descended from the lamp room and found her sitting at his table, hands folded neatly, dark hair loose as it had been before the children were born, he did not scream, and he did not weep. He sat down across from her, bone-tired, because even a counterfeit fashioned in her image was preferable to the empty chair that had haunted him across three years of identical evenings.

"You kept the light on," she said. Her voice was honey poured into a wound.

"I always keep the light on."

She smiled—her exact smile, down to the small crookedness at the left corner—and something in his chest broke open that had been sealed since the water came. "You look older," she said. Not unkindly.

"I am older. You didn't get to be."

She reached across the table and her hand was warm, and that was the first thing that truly frightened him—not her presence, but the warmth of her, the solid fact of it, while he himself grew less substantial by the day. He woke each morning now with whole hours already hollowed out, remnants of the night's dreaming gone before he could reach them. He found entries in the logbook in his own handwriting describing things he had no memory of recording. *Daughter's name—* one began, and then trailed into blank space, a dark gap he could not cross. He had a daughter. He knew this. He could see small shoes beside a door he could no longer color in his mind, an indeterminate rectangle bleeding into the gray of everything else.

Each night, Mara grew more solid. He grew less. The arithmetic tormented him in the hours before dawn: she was being *built* from his disintegration, assembled from materials the darkness had stripped from him and recast in her shape. He was being consumed, and the consumption wore her face, and he let it.

---

He climbed into the lamp housing on the fourteenth night, against the instruction, against the cold dread that had taken permanent residence in his sternum. Inside, past the ancient lens, he found the mechanism—old, older than the collapse, older than the company that had contracted him, older perhaps than the grief that had made him contractable. The light did not project outward. It gathered, and it fed downward, into a shaft bored through the rock beneath the tower's foundation, down into the drowned and lightless dark. It pulsed with a rhythm that was not the machine's own—slow, deliberate, patient as centuries. Something below was keeping time. Something below was breathing. The light resonated with that breath, and the understanding arrived without mercy: the light was made of him.

A logbook older than his own lay discarded in the housing, its pages dense with names—forty-one keepers before him. None transferred. None returned inland. Beside each name, inscribed in the same hand, a single word: *Consumed.* Beside the forty-second name—his own, entered in ink that had not dried long—no final entry, only an estimation: *Vane, Aldous. Estimated depletion—* and then a date. Eleven days out.

"You weren't supposed to come up here," said Wren's voice, now stripped to a whisper, and the warmth had evacuated it entirely, leaving something ancient and starving in its place. It no longer sounded like her. It had never been her.

"There were no ships," he said, and his voice was steady in the way a man's voice is steady when he has gone past the point of fear into something colder. "There was never a ship. It was always *this*."

"There is always this," it replied. The hunger in it was immense—continental, geologic, the appetite of something that had been waiting long before the waters rose. "The world floods. The grieving drift toward the edge. And grief is such clean fuel. Slow. Willing. Self-replenishing." A pause, almost affectionate in its horror. "You have been very good, Aldous. You have given so much and asked for so little in return. Most of them fight. You only forgot."

---

He descended with a sledgehammer—the last deliberate act available to him—intending to destroy the lamp and deny the thing its final eleven days of feeding. A cold and furious desire moved through him, clean as a blade: he would give it nothing more of himself. He would choose the darkness on his own terms. But Mara stood at the top of the hundred and forty steps, and she was solid, and she was waiting.

"Don't," she said.

"Tell me you're not real." His voice fractured on the last word. "Tell me you're only the part of me it hasn't finished eating yet. Tell me you're bait."

"Yes," she said, and her willingness to agree cut deeper than any protestation could have. "I know what I am. But Aldous—I am also the last of her. Everything you remember of me is living in the light now. It drew it out of you, piece by piece, and it put it *here*, into this shape. If you break the lamp, I don't go free." She held his gaze without flinching. "I go out. There would be nothing of her left anywhere in the world—not in you, not in me, not anywhere. You would have killed me twice." She stepped closer, warmth radiating from her like a low fever, a sickly, beautiful heat that was the cruelest thing he had ever felt. "Or you keep the light on. Keep me. It's eleven days. What is eleven more days of forgetting against the permanent dark of never seeing her face again?"

He held the sledge at his side, hovering at the boundary of a reason that had been eroding for fourteen nights. Below him, the sea breathed its slow, ancient breath, and the shape of the trap was fully visible to him now—had always been visible, he supposed, to anyone with the courage to look squarely at it. The creature did not feed on loneliness. It fed on love. On the specifically human willingness to be destroyed in increments rather than release the dead. He understood this. He saw the mechanism clearly, the way he had once seen the mechanism of floods—the mathematics of it, the inevitability. Understanding did not constitute escape. It never had.

He set the sledge down.

He lit the light. He lit it the next night, and the night after that, and each time he watched Mara grow more present, more exact, more terrible in her exactness. Each night he surrendered a little more of whatever architecture was left inside him. The last remnant of red faded from the door he could no longer find in his mind, and still he fed the light, still he sat across from what the darkness had built from his ruins and called it by her name. On the horizon, barely visible in the thinning fog, a transport vessel moved toward the coast—a new contract worker, already dispatched, already scheduled. The machine did not pause. The appetite of the deep was not a thing that could be sated or reasoned with or mourned. It was simply ongoing, the way the sea was ongoing, the way loss was ongoing, consuming the next willing keeper before the current one had finished disappearing.

## Editorial Documentation

### Line-Edit & Proofread Notes

**Tonal Consistency (Darkness Reinforced at Sentence Level)**

The following targeted interventions were made to ensure no phrase inadvertently softened the story's register:

1. **Opening paragraph:** "forgotten in a graveyard" → "abandoned in a graveyard" (subtler, less gothic-cliché; the word *abandoned* carries desolation rather than drama). "thick as miasma" → "thick as old blood" (more visceral, less literary-distancing). Added: "He had stopped imagining whose face it might be. That was how you survived out here. You stopped imagining." — This closes the paragraph with active psychological bleakness rather than passive description.

2. **Lamp-lighting paragraph:** "the death rattle of a long-abandoned hope" → "the death rattle of an exhausted animal" (more physical, less abstractly poetic; grounds the horror in body rather than concept). "the poor were permitted" → "the expendable were permitted" (sharper class-horror register; *poor* implies mere poverty, *expendable* implies systemic disposal). Added "Not that there are rocks I haven't already memorized by grief" — *by grief* rather than *by now* ties the mundane complaint back to the emotional core.

3. **Wren's voice:** "warm as decay" retained and sharpened in later revelation. "gentle as a nurse" → "gentle as a sedative" (removes the human caregiver implication; a sedative administers calm in order to incapacitate, which is tonally appropriate). The phrase "keep yourself company" now explicitly reread as "the grammar of a cage" — giving the reader the interpretive key without over-explaining.

4. **Mara's return:** "voice like honey in winter" → "voice like honey poured into a wound" (honey in winter implies sweetness offset by cold; honey in a wound implies something that soothes *while* it corrupts, which is the precise action Mara performs on Aldous). "something in his chest broke open that had been sealed for three years" → "something in his chest broke open that had been sealed since the water came" — tying his emotional wound to the literal flood grounds the personal in the political catastrophe.

5. **Memory erosion sequence:** The logbook entry "*Daughter's name—*" trailing into blankness was sharpened from a vague existential note into something more immediately horrifying — he knows the *category* (daughter) but cannot recover the *content* (name, face). "an indeterminate rectangle silently awaiting" → "an indeterminate rectangle bleeding into the gray of everything else" (*bleeding* is active decay; *silently awaiting* was passive and faintly ominous in an almost peaceful way — wrong register).

6. **Lamp housing discovery:** "detritus of a forgotten time" removed — it was decorative and distancing. The mechanism description was tightened to remove any sense of wonder; this is not a revelation of the sublime but of predation. "dangerously clear" removed — the adverb hedged; the construction was recast as flat declarative: "the understanding arrived without mercy." The phrase "the light was made of him" was retained as a paragraph-ending sentence precisely because of its flat, unadorned horror.

7. **Wren's unmasking:** "tainted with something ancient and malicious" → cut; shown instead through: "the warmth had evacuated it entirely, leaving something ancient and starving in its place." *Starving* is more active and specific than *malicious* — evil is a category, hunger is a force. "the tone of insatiable hunger breaking through the veneer of civility" → cut as redundant telling; the dialogue itself now carries this weight. "almost affectionate" retained — the most disturbing word in the passage.

8. **Climactic confrontation:** "his last act of will" → "the last deliberate act available to him" (avoids the heroic connotation of *will*; frames agency as a narrowing corridor rather than a noble assertion). "A cold desire surged through him" → "A cold and furious desire moved through him, clean as a blade" (surged is melodramatic; *moved through him, clean as a blade* is quieter and more precise about the quality of the feeling — not hot rage but cold resolve). Mara's speech was restructured for rhythm: short declarative sentences interspersed with longer ones to control reader breathing and prevent the passage from becoming emotionally manipulative rather than devastating.

9. **Final paragraph:** "frantically feeding that which had already consumed him" — removed *frantically*; the horror of the ending is in its quietness, not its frenzy. The replacement construction emphasizes ritual repetition: "still he fed the light, still he sat across from what the darkness had built from his ruins and called it by her name." The new contract worker's arrival was reframed as a vessel on the horizon rather than a scheduled note, making the cycle feel oceanic and inevitable rather than bureaucratic. Final sentence restructured to end on "disappearing" rather than "appetite of the deep" — the last word belongs to Aldous, to the human being being erased, not to the predator consuming him.

**Proofreading Corrections**

- Comma splice resolved in lamp housing paragraph (two independent clauses joined without conjunction).
- "ever more-present" → "more present" (hyphen error corrected).
- "He acquiesced" removed; Mara is not acquiescing, she is offering a devastating honesty — "she said" restored, with the weight carried by content not speech tag.
- "hovering on the edge of reason" → "hovering at the boundary of a reason that had been eroding for fourteen nights" (not only corrects the cliché but makes the erosion literal and time-stamped).
- Consistent past-tense narration verified throughout; one present-tense intrusion in the critical notes section did not appear in story proper.
- Em-dashes standardized throughout; two instances of double-hyphen converted.
- Paragraph spacing and section breaks normalized for submission format.

**Voice & Rhythm Assessment**

The final version maintains a consistent third-person close narration that stays within Aldous's increasingly compromised consciousness without ever becoming unreliable to the point of obscuring the horror. Sentence rhythm alternates between longer, clause-heavy constructions during moments of psychological complexity and short declarative sentences at moments of revelation, producing a controlled tension that pays off in the climax. The prose avoids both the florid excess of gothic pastiche and the clinical flatness of hard SF, occupying a register appropriate to literary horror: precise, weighted, and without ornament that doesn't earn its place.

The darkness is reinforced not through accumulation of dark imagery alone but through the systematic removal of any grammatical or rhetorical structure that implies escape, relief, or redemption. Questions in the text go unanswered. Hopes are named in order to be extinguished. The final sentence refuses comfort, refuses irony, and refuses the reader the distance of metaphor: it simply describes an ongoing process of erasure, and ends.

## PART 1: PREMISE & WORLD-BUILDING

**Logline:** On a drowning Earth where the seas have swallowed the coasts, a lighthouse keeper maintaining an obsolete beacon discovers that the light he tends is not warning ships away from the rocks — it is calling something up from the deep, and he has been fuel for it all along.

**The World:** A near-future post-collapse Earth. Sea levels rose faster than any model predicted after a cascade of methane releases. Coastal cities are archaeology now, visible as green-black shapes under brown water. What remains of civilization has retreated inland into administrative "Uplands." The old coasts are a legal nowhere — abandoned, unmonitored, kept alive only by automated infrastructure and a skeleton crew of contract laborers too poor or too broken to leave.

The lighthouses were never re-purposed for ships (there is no shipping left). Officially they run on inertia — bureaucratic zombie-programs no one canceled. The protagonist believes he keeps a light for vessels that will never come. He is wrong about what the light is for.

**The Impossible Thing:** The light does not shine outward. It shines *down*. Beneath the risen sea, the flooding woke something old — not alien, but *native*, a thing that predates the fossil record, that the carbon age simply thawed loose. The lighthouse "keepers" are not employees. They are a feeding schedule. The beacon converts a living human's neural activity — slow attrition of memory, self, and years — into a signal the deep thing consumes. Loneliness is not a side effect of the job. It is the extraction process.

---

## PART 2: MAIN CHARACTERS

- **Aldous Vane** — the keeper. Fifty-one, a former hydrology engineer who signed the contract after his family drowned in the Retreat. He came to the coast to be near the water that killed them, a private penance. He is precise, self-punishing, and quietly suicidal in a way he'd never admit. His grief is the door the story walks through.

- **The Voice ("Signal-6")** — what speaks to Aldous through the beacon's old comms channel. It presents as a company relay operator named "Wren," warm and administrative, gently discouraging him from asking questions. It is the thing in the deep wearing a friendly interface.

- **Mara** — Aldous's dead wife, whose memory the light is slowly harvesting. As the story progresses she appears to him, reconstructed from the very memories being drained. She becomes more vivid the more of her he loses — the cruelest mechanics of the extraction.

---

## PART 3: BEAT-BY-BEAT OUTLINE

1. **Establishing the ritual.** Aldous performs the nightly lighting. Fog, rust, isolation. The world's death rendered mundane. Comfort in routine.
2. **First wrongness.** He notices the lamp's beam bends downward into the water instead of sweeping the horizon. He tells himself it's a lens defect.
3. **The Voice.** "Wren" checks in via comms. Reassuring, incurious, subtly steering him away from the malfunction.
4. **Mara appears.** He begins seeing his wife in the tower. He assumes grief, exhaustion. She is tender. He is grateful — the story's only warmth, and it is bait.
5. **Escalating loss.** He can't remember his daughter's name. Can't remember the color of his old front door. Each forgotten thing makes Mara sharper, closer.
6. **The discovery.** He climbs into the lamp housing and sees the truth: the light pulses in rhythm with something enormous moving beneath the sea. The logbook of previous keepers — all vanished, none reassigned. He was never keeping ships safe.
7. **Confrontation with the Voice.** "Wren" drops the pretense only enough to explain nothing. It doesn't need to lie anymore. Aldous is nearly empty.
8. **The choice.** He can smash the lamp — but Mara begs him not to, because she only exists inside the light now. To end the feeding is to kill the last of his wife a second time.
9. **The dark ending (earned, unsettling).** He chooses her. He keeps the light. He forgets on purpose, willingly, feeding the deep to hold a ghost. The final beat: a new contract worker's transport approaches the coast — a replacement already scheduled. The machine never stops. Neither does the appetite.

## PART 4: COMPLETE FIRST DRAFT

### THE ATTRITION LIGHT

The sea had taken everything worth taking, and now it only wanted the small things.

Aldous Vane climbed the tower at dusk, as he had climbed it every dusk for three years, and the iron rang under his boots the way a bell rings when no one is listening. One hundred and forty steps. He counted them not because he could forget the number but because the counting was something to hold, and out here a man learned quickly that he needed things to hold. The fog came up off the water in the evenings, thick and brown-gold, and it pressed against the glass of the lamp room like a face.

He lit the beacon at 6:14, precisely, because precision was the last dignity the poor were permitted. The lamp woke with a low sound, almost a breath, and the beam swept out across the drowned world — across the flat brown ocean that had been, once, a city of nine million people. Somewhere under that water was a street he had lived on. Somewhere under that water was a small blue house with a red door, though tonight, standing at the glass, he found he could not quite remember the color of the door. Red, he told himself. It was red. He wrote it in the logbook out of some instinct he could not name: *Door was red.* Then he felt foolish and crossed it out.

"Evening, Aldous." The comms crackled, and the woman's voice came through warm as it always did. Wren, from the relay station inland — though he had never seen the relay station, and had begun, lately, to wonder if it existed at all. "Nominal night on your end?"

"Nominal," he said. He hesitated. "The beam's doing that thing again."

"What thing is that?"

"Bending. Down. It sweeps the horizon and then it — dips. Into the water. Like it's looking for something under there instead of warning ships off the rocks." He laughed, or made the shape of a laugh. "Not that there are ships. Not that there are rocks I haven't already got memorized."

There was a pause on the line, and in the pause he heard something. A vast, slow, patient sound, like the sea inhaling. Then Wren again, gentle as a nurse. "Lens calibration drifts in the damp, that's all. Don't climb up into the housing to fix it, all right? That's not in your contract. You just keep the light on. That's all we've ever needed you to do. Keep the light on and keep yourself company."

*Keep yourself company.* He turned the phrase over long after the channel went quiet. It had the shape of kindness. It was only later he understood it was an instruction.

---

Mara came back on the ninth night of the fog.

He knew she was not real. He was a rational man; he had built flood models once, had trusted numbers over hope and been proven right in the worst possible way. He knew that grief made ghosts, that the mind under enough silence would manufacture company from whatever it had loved. So when he came down from the lamp room and found her sitting at his small table, her hands folded, her dark hair loose the way she'd worn it before the children were born, he did not scream and he did not weep. He simply sat down across from her, because he was so tired, and because even a lie shaped like Mara was better than the empty chair.

"You kept the light on," she said.

"I always keep the light on."

She smiled at him, and it was her smile exactly, down to the small crookedness of it, and something in his chest broke open that had been sealed for three years. "You look older," she said, not unkindly.

"I am older. You didn't get to be."

She reached across the table and took his hand, and her hand was warm, and that was the first thing that frightened him — not that she had come, but that she was *warm*, warmer each night, more solid, while he himself felt each day a little thinner, a little further away. He would wake and not remember what he had eaten. He would find entries in the logbook in his own hand about things he could not recall writing. *Daughter's name —* one entry began, and then nothing, a blank, as if the pen had refused. He stared at that blank for a long time. He had a daughter. He knew he had a daughter. He could see her small shoes by the red door, but the door was not red anymore in his memory, it was only a door, a gray rectangle of nothing, and the shoes were beside it but the feet that had filled them had no name and soon, he realized with a cold and clinical horror, would have no shoes.

And every night Mara grew clearer. That was the arithmetic he could not stop solving. The more he lost, the more of her there was. She was being *built* out of him.

---

He climbed into the lamp housing on the fourteenth night, against instruction, because he had finally understood that instruction was the enemy.

Inside, past the great lens, the mechanism was nothing he had been trained on. It was old — older than the collapse, older than the company, older, he suspected, than the counting of years. The light did not project outward at all. It gathered. It fed down through a shaft cut into the rock beneath the tower, down into the drowned dark, and it pulsed — and he saw now that it pulsed in a rhythm, a slow and enormous rhythm, and that the rhythm was not the machine's. Something below was keeping time. Something below was *breathing*, and the light was breathing with it, and the light was made of him.

There was a logbook in the housing older than his own. He read the names of the keepers before him. There were forty-one. None had been transferred. None had gone home. Beside each name, in the same patient administrative hand, was written a single word: *Consumed.* And beside the forty-second name — his own — the entry was not yet finished. It read: *Vane, Aldous. Estimated depletion —* and then a date. Eleven days out.

"You weren't supposed to come up here," said Wren's voice, from a speaker rusted into the wall. But it did not sound like Wren anymore. It had stopped bothering. Underneath the warmth was the other thing, the vast slow inhale, and it spoke to him now not as a woman but as an appetite that had learned the manners of one.

"There were no ships," Aldous said. "There was never a ship. It was always this."

"There is always this," it agreed. "The world floods. The lonely come to the edge of it to grieve. And grief is such a clean fuel. So slow. So willing." A pause, almost tender. "You have been very good, Aldous. You have given so much and asked so little. Most of them fight. You only forgot."

---

He went down to smash the lamp. It was the only thing left to do, and he found, dimly, beneath the emptiness, that he wanted to do it — wanted to deny the thing its last eleven days of him, to go out with the one act of will he had left.

Mara was standing at the top of the hundred and forty steps.

"Don't," she said.

"You're not real." His voice cracked. "You're the part of me it hasn't eaten yet. You're bait."

"I know," she said, and her honesty was worse than any pleading. "I know what I am. But Aldous — I'm also the last of her. Everything you remember of me lives in the light now. It took it out of you and it put it *here*, into me. If you break the lamp, I don't go free. I go out. There won't be anything left of her anywhere, not in you, not in the world. You'll have killed me twice." She stepped down toward him, warm and whole and dying by design. "Or you can keep the light on. You can keep me. It's only eleven days. What is eleven more days of forgetting, against never seeing me again?"

He stood on the iron stair with the sledge in his hands, and below him the sea breathed, and he understood the trap completely — understood that this had been the shape of it all along, that the thing in the deep did not feed on loneliness but on love, on the terrible willingness of a man to be devoured rather than let go of the dead. He understood, and it did not help. It never does.

He set the sledge down.

The sea had taken everything worth taking, and now it only wanted the small things.

Aldous Vane climbed the tower at dusk, as he had climbed it every dusk for three years, and the iron rang under his boots the way a bell rings when no one is listening. One hundred and forty steps. He counted them not because he could forget the number but because the counting was something to hold, and out here a man learned quickly that he needed things to hold. The fog came up off the water in the evenings, thick and brown-gold, and it pressed against the glass of the lamp room like a face.

He lit the beacon at 6:14, precisely, because precision was the last dignity the poor were permitted. The lamp woke with a low sound, almost a breath, and the beam swept out across the drowned world — across the flat brown ocean that had been, once, a city of nine million people. Somewhere under that water was a street he had lived on. Somewhere under that water was a small blue house with a red door, though tonight, standing at the glass, he found he could not quite remember the color of the door. Red, he told himself. It was red. He wrote it in the logbook out of some instinct he could not name: *Door was red.* Then he felt foolish and crossed it out.

"Evening, Aldous." The comms crackled, and the woman's voice came through warm as it always did. Wren, from the relay station inland — though he had never seen the relay station, and had begun, lately, to wonder if it existed at all. "Nominal night on your end?"

"Nominal," he said. He hesitated. "The beam's doing that thing again."

"What thing is that?"

"Bending. Down. It sweeps the horizon and then it — dips. Into the water. Like it's looking for something under there instead of warning ships off the rocks." He laughed, or made the shape of a laugh. "Not that there are ships. Not that there are rocks I haven't already got memorized."

There was a pause on the line, and in the pause he heard something. A vast, slow, patient sound, like the sea inhaling. Then Wren again, gentle as a nurse. "Lens calibration drifts in the damp, that's all. Don't climb up into the housing to fix it, all right? That's not in your contract. You just keep the light on. That's all we've ever needed you to do. Keep the light on and keep yourself company."

*Keep yourself company.* He turned the phrase over long after the channel went quiet. It had the shape of kindness. It was only later he understood it was an instruction.

Mara came back on the ninth night of the fog.

He knew she was not real. He was a rational man; he had built flood models once, had trusted numbers over hope and been proven right in the worst possible way. He knew that grief made ghosts, that the mind under enough silence would manufacture company from whatever it had loved. So when he came down from the lamp room and found her sitting at his small table, her hands folded, her dark hair loose the way she'd worn it before the children were born, he did not scream and he did not weep. He simply sat down across from her, because he was so tired, and because even a lie shaped like Mara was better than the empty chair.

"You kept the light on," she said.

"I always keep the light on."

She smiled at him, and it was her smile exactly, down to the small crookedness of it, and something in his chest broke open that had been sealed for three years. "You look older," she said, not unkindly.

"I am older. You didn't get to be."

She reached across the table and took his hand, and her hand was warm, and that was the first thing that frightened him — not that she had come, but that she was *warm*, warmer each night, more solid, while he himself felt each day a little thinner, a little further away. He would wake and not remember what he had eaten. He would find entries in the logbook in his own hand about things he could not recall writing. *Daughter's name —* one entry began, and then nothing, a blank, as if the pen had refused. He stared at that blank for a long time. He had a daughter. He knew he had a daughter. He could see her small shoes by the red door, but the door was not red anymore in his memory, it was only a door, a gray rectangle of nothing, and the shoes were beside it but the feet that had filled them had no name and soon, he realized with a cold and clinical horror, would have no shoes.

And every night Mara grew clearer. That was the arithmetic he could not stop solving. The more he lost, the more of her there was. She was being *built* out of him.

Something below was keeping time. Something below was *breathing*, and the light was breathing with it, and the light was made of him.

There was a logbook in the housing older than his own. He read the names of the keepers before him. There were forty-one. None had been transferred. None had gone home. "You weren't supposed to come up here," said Wren's voice, from a speaker rusted into the wall. But it did not sound like Wren anymore. It had stopped bothering. Underneath the warmth was the other thing, the vast slow inhale, and it spoke to him now not as a woman but as an appetite that had learned the manners of one.

That was how you survived out here. You stopped imagining.

He lit the beacon at 6:14, precisely. Precision was the last dignity the expendable were permitted. The lamp shuddered to life with a low sound—not mechanical, not quite—something between a sigh and the death rattle of an exhausted animal. The beam swept out over the drowned world: a flat expanse of brown water that had once been a city of nine million people. Somewhere beneath that water was a street he had lived on. Somewhere beneath that brown surface was a small house with a door he could no longer remember the color of. Red, he told himself. It was red. He reached for the certainty and found only wet sand. He scribbled in the logbook: *Door was red.* The words looked hollow even as the ink dried, and he crossed them out in a single hard stroke.

"Evening, Aldous." The comms crackled, and the woman's voice drifted through—warm as decay. Wren, she called herself. From the relay station inland, though he had never seen it, and lately he had begun to doubt it existed at all. "Nominal night on your end?"

"Nominal," he said, though the word tasted like something left too long in the dark. He hesitated. "The beam's doing that thing again."

"What thing?"

"Bending. Downward. It still sweeps the horizon, but it dips—like it's searching for something beneath the surface instead of warning ships off." He laughed, the sound cracking at its edges, something brittle that couldn't quite connect to the man producing it. "Not that there are ships. Not that there are rocks I haven't already memorized by grief."

A silence filled the line. In it, he heard something—a vast, patient sound, the slow respiration of the sea itself. Then Wren, gentle as a sedative: "Lens calibration drifts in the damp. Don't go up into the housing to fix it—that's not in your contract. Just keep the light on. That's all we ever needed you to do. Keep the light on and keep yourself company."

*Keep yourself company.* The phrase twisted in his mind long after the channel went silent, curling into something that no longer resembled kindness. It was the grammar of a cage. A door left open just enough to suggest freedom while the walls held.

---

Mara returned on the ninth night of the fog.

He knew she wasn't real—or at least he believed he knew this, which was not the same thing. He was a rational man. A hydrology engineer: all numbers, all models, all forecast overlaid with despair. He had trusted equations over hope and been proven right in the most devastating way possible. Grief manufactured ghosts; the mind would cannibalize whatever it had left to build itself company. So when he descended from the lamp room and found her sitting at his table, hands folded neatly, dark hair loose as it had been before the children were born, he did not scream, and he did not weep. He sat down across from her, bone-tired, because even a counterfeit fashioned in her image was preferable to the empty chair that had haunted him across three years of identical evenings.

"You kept the light on," she said. Her voice was honey poured into a wound.

"I always keep the light on."

She smiled—her exact smile, down to the small crookedness at the left corner—and something in his chest broke open that had been sealed since the water came. "You look older," she said. Not unkindly.

"I am older. You didn't get to be."

She reached across the table and her hand was warm, and that was the first thing that truly frightened him—not her presence, but the warmth of her, the solid fact of it, while he himself grew less substantial by the day. He woke each morning now with whole hours already hollowed out, remnants of the night's dreaming gone before he could reach them. He found entries in the logbook in his own handwriting describing things he had no memory of recording. *Daughter's name—* one began, and then trailed into blank space, a dark gap he could not cross. He had a daughter. He knew this. He could see small shoes beside a door he could no longer color in his mind, an indeterminate rectangle bleeding into the gray of everything else.

Each night, Mara grew more solid. He grew less. The arithmetic tormented him in the hours before dawn: she was being *built* from his disintegration, assembled from materials the darkness had stripped from him and recast in her shape. He was being consumed, and the consumption wore her face, and he let it.

---

He climbed into the lamp housing on the fourteenth night, against the instruction, against the cold dread that had taken permanent residence in his sternum. Inside, past the ancient lens, he found the mechanism—old, older than the collapse, older than the company that had contracted him, older perhaps than the grief that had made him contractable. The light did not project outward. It gathered, and it fed downward, into a shaft bored through the rock beneath the tower's foundation, down into the drowned and lightless dark. It pulsed with a rhythm that was not the machine's own—slow, deliberate, patient as centuries. Something below was keeping time. Something below was breathing. The light resonated with that breath, and the understanding arrived without mercy: the light was made of him.

A logbook older than his own lay discarded in the housing, its pages dense with names—forty-one keepers before him. None transferred. None returned inland. Beside each name, inscribed in the same hand, a single word: *Consumed.* Beside the forty-second name—his own, entered in ink that had not dried long—no final entry, only an estimation: *Vane, Aldous. Estimated depletion—* and then a date. Eleven days out.

"You weren't supposed to come up here," said Wren's voice, now stripped to a whisper, and the warmth had evacuated it entirely, leaving something ancient and starving in its place. It no longer sounded like her. It had never been her.

"There were no ships," he said, and his voice was steady in the way a man's voice is steady when he has gone past the point of fear into something colder. "There was never a ship. It was always *this*."

"There is always this," it replied. The hunger in it was immense—continental, geologic, the appetite of something that had been waiting long before the waters rose. "The world floods. The grieving drift toward the edge. And grief is such clean fuel. Slow. Willing. Self-replenishing." A pause, almost affectionate in its horror. "You have been very good, Aldous. You have given so much and asked for so little in return. Most of them fight. You only forgot."

---

He descended with a sledgehammer—the last deliberate act available to him—intending to destroy the lamp and deny the thing its final eleven days of feeding. A cold and furious desire moved through him, clean as a blade: he would give it nothing more of himself. He would choose the darkness on his own terms. But Mara stood at the top of the hundred and forty steps, and she was solid, and she was waiting.

"Don't," she said.

"Tell me you're not real." His voice fractured on the last word. "Tell me you're only the part of me it hasn't finished eating yet. Tell me you're bait."

"Yes," she said, and her willingness to agree cut deeper than any protestation could have. "I know what I am. But Aldous—I am also the last of her. Everything you remember of me is living in the light now. It drew it out of you, piece by piece, and it put it *here*, into this shape. If you break the lamp, I don't go free." She held his gaze without flinching. "I go out. There would be nothing of her left anywhere in the world—not in you, not in me, not anywhere. You would have killed me twice." She stepped closer, warmth radiating from her like a low fever, a sickly, beautiful heat that was the cruelest thing he had ever felt. "Or you keep the light on. Keep me. It's eleven days. What is eleven more days of forgetting against the permanent dark of never seeing her face again?"

He held the sledge at his side, hovering at the boundary of a reason that had been eroding for fourteen nights. Below him, the sea breathed its slow, ancient breath, and the shape of the trap was fully visible to him now—had always been visible, he supposed, to anyone with the courage to look squarely at it. The creature did not feed on loneliness. It fed on love. On the specifically human willingness to be destroyed in increments rather than release the dead. He understood this. He saw the mechanism clearly, the way he had once seen the mechanism of floods—the mathematics of it, the inevitability. Understanding did not constitute escape. It never had.

He set the sledge down.

He lit the light. He lit it the next night, and the night after that, and each time he watched Mara grow more present, more exact, more terrible in her exactness. Each night he surrendered a little more of whatever architecture was left inside him. The last remnant of red faded from the door he could no longer find in his mind, and still he fed the light, still he sat across from what the darkness had built from his ruins and called it by her name. On the horizon, barely visible in the thinning fog, a transport vessel moved toward the coast—a new contract worker, already dispatched, already scheduled. The machine did not pause. The appetite of the deep was not a thing that could be sated or reasoned with or mourned. It was simply ongoing, the way the sea was ongoing, the way loss was ongoing, consuming the next willing keeper before the current one had finished disappearing.
