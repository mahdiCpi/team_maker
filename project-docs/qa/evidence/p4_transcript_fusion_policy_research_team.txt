## ABSTRACT

The fusion energy policy landscape of 2026 represents a historic inflection point in the trajectory of clean energy development. This literature review synthesizes current policy developments across four structurally transformative domains: (1) the institutionalization of public-private partnership models in the United States, anchored by the Department of Energy's Milestone-Based Fusion Development Program; (2) the emergence of purpose-built regulatory frameworks, most notably the Nuclear Regulatory Commission's proposed Part 185 rulemaking; (3) the deepening of international cooperation architectures that increasingly incorporate private sector actors alongside state entities; and (4) the crystallization of geopolitical competition in fusion technology development. Drawing on policy documents, government announcements, regulatory filings, and scholarly analysis from the 2022–2026 period, this review identifies the "commercialization transition" as the defining analytical frame for understanding contemporary fusion energy governance — a movement from a domain dominated by government-funded, scientifically-oriented megaprojects toward a hybrid ecosystem in which private capital, government strategic partnerships, and international cooperation frameworks coexist, interact, and increasingly compete. The review concludes by identifying key gaps in existing scholarship and directions for future research.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Fusion energy — the process of combining light atomic nuclei to release energy, replicating the mechanism that powers the sun — has occupied a unique and paradoxical position in energy policy discourse for over seven decades. Long characterized by the quip that commercial fusion is "always 30 years away," the field has undergone a series of developments in the 2020s that have fundamentally altered both its technical trajectory and its policy environment (Fusion Industry Association [FIA], 2024). The achievement of scientific energy gain at the National Ignition Facility (NIF) in December 2022 — the first laboratory demonstration in which fusion reactions produced more energy than the laser energy delivered to the fuel target — provided both a scientific milestone and a powerful political signal that catalyzed a new generation of policy responses at national and international levels (U.S. Department of Energy [DOE], Office of Science, 2023a).

By 2026, the policy environment for fusion energy has been transformed across multiple dimensions simultaneously. The United States has institutionalized a public-private partnership model for fusion commercialization that draws explicit lessons from the Commercial Crew Program in space and the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) model in defense innovation (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) is finalizing what will be the world's first purpose-built regulatory framework for commercial fusion facilities, moving from a regulatory vacuum that posed existential risks to commercialization toward a structured, risk-proportionate governance architecture (NRC, 2023). The United Kingdom has committed to a national fusion demonstration program anchored by a specific site and a 2040s delivery timeline (UK Atomic Energy Authority [UKAEA], 2024). And the geopolitical dimensions of fusion development — long subordinated to the cooperative international science framework centered on ITER — have become increasingly salient as both China and the United States have elevated fusion to strategic technology priority status (FIA, 2024; International Atomic Energy Agency [IAEA], 2024).

This literature review aims to synthesize these developments into a structured, academically grounded account of the fusion energy policy landscape in 2026. It is organized into five thematic sections: (1) the public-private partnership revolution in U.S. fusion policy; (2) regulatory framework development and the transition from void to structure; (3) a comparative analysis of national fusion strategies; (4) international cooperation architectures and geopolitical competition; and (5) cross-cutting themes, including workforce development, supply chain governance, and public acceptance. Each section identifies key findings, analytical tensions, and implications for scholarly research. The review concludes with a synthesis of major findings and a research agenda for the emerging field of fusion energy governance studies.

A note on sources and scope: This review draws principally on policy documents, government reports, regulatory filings, and analyses from the 2022–2026 period. Where specific 2026 policy documents are not yet publicly available, the review relies on documented roadmaps, anticipated regulatory timelines, and programmatic evolution trajectories as stated in authoritative sources. All citations reflect the attribution conventions of the sources analyzed and are formatted according to standard academic referencing practice.

## 2. THE PUBLIC-PRIVATE PARTNERSHIP REVOLUTION IN U.S. FUSION POLICY

### 2.1 The Paradigm Shift in Federal Engagement Models

The most consequential structural development in U.S. fusion policy in the 2023–2026 period is the institutionalization of public-private partnership as the primary vehicle for fusion energy commercialization. This represents a fundamental departure from the previous paradigm in which the federal government served as effectively the sole funder, operator, and intellectual driver of fusion research in the United States — a paradigm rooted in the Big Science tradition established in the post-World War II era and exemplified by the multi-decade, multi-billion-dollar ITER project (McCray, 2010; Clery, 2013).

The DOE's Milestone-Based Fusion Development Program, announced in May 2023 with $46 million in initial awards to eight private fusion companies, operationalized a philosophy that had been gestating in policy circles since the early 2020s: that the private fusion sector had matured sufficiently — in terms of capital access, technical expertise, and organizational capability — to serve as a genuine partner in, rather than merely a recipient of, federal research investment (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). The program's structural architecture merits detailed scholarly attention.

**Cost-Share Architecture:** The minimum 50% private co-investment requirement ensures that federal funds leverage rather than substitute for private capital. This distinguishes the Milestone Program from traditional research grants and creates aligned incentive structures between public and private partners. For companies such as Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS), which received up to $10 million from the program while simultaneously commanding over $1.8 billion in private investment by 2024, the federal award functions less as a financial lifeline than as a technical validation and reputational credentialing signal to private markets (FIA, 2024; DOE, Office of Science, 2023b).

**Performance-Based Payment Triggers:** Unlike block grants or fixed-schedule contracts, the Milestone Program releases funds upon demonstrated achievement of pre-specified technical, engineering, regulatory, and commercial milestones. This performance-based architecture creates accountability mechanisms absent from traditional research funding and aligns with the "results-oriented government" reform tradition in U.S. federal procurement practice (National Science and Technology Council [NSTC], 2022).

**Regulatory Engagement as a Program Milestone:** The explicit inclusion of NRC pre-application meeting attendance as a program milestone is a notable policy innovation, institutionalizing regulatory pathway development as a parallel track to technical development rather than a deferred consideration (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b; NRC, 2023). This reflects awareness — now documented in the policy literature — that the commercialization bottleneck for fusion may ultimately be regulatory as much as technical (Hawkins & Laidlaw, 2023).

**Technology Portfolio Diversity:** The eight first-cohort awardees represent five distinct fusion confinement approaches: high-field tokamak (Commonwealth Fusion Systems), field-reversed configuration (TAE Technologies, Helion Energy), stellarator (Princeton Stellarators), mirror configuration (Realta Fusion), inertial confinement (Focused Energy, Xcimer Energy), and Z-pinch (Zap Energy). This portfolio approach reflects a deliberate policy hedge against the possibility that any single approach will emerge as the dominant commercial technology, maximizing the probability that at least one pathway achieves commercial viability within the relevant policy timeframe (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b; FIA, 2024).

**Other Transaction Authority (OTA) Contracting:** The program's use of OTA enables non-traditional contracting flexibility that standard procurement law would not permit — including the ability to engage companies without traditional cost accounting systems, to structure milestone payments around technical demonstrations rather than input-based deliverables, and to incorporate proprietary information protections appropriate for commercially sensitive fusion technology (NSTC, 2022; DOE, Office of Science, 2023b).

### 2.2 First Cohort Assessment and Second Cohort Development (2025–2026)

The Milestone-Based Program's anticipated second cohort for the 2025–2026 timeframe — with expanded funding levels subject to Congressional appropriations — represents a critical test of the program's programmatic logic and institutional durability (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). Several dimensions of this evolution warrant scholarly attention.

**Scaling Dynamics:** The first cohort's $46 million across eight companies, averaging $5.75 million per company, is modest relative to the capital requirements of advanced fusion development. Individual companies in the FIA's 2024 survey reported having raised hundreds of millions to billions in private capital (FIA, 2024). The second cohort's anticipated expansion likely reflects both the DOE's assessment of first-cohort program effectiveness and congressional interest signals following the NIF ignition achievement's sustained political salience. The direction of scaling — in terms of both total program funding and per-company award levels — will serve as a significant indicator of the federal government's long-term commitment to this partnership model.

**Cohort Graduation Logic:** As first-cohort companies advance through milestones, the second cohort likely targets companies at earlier development stages or pursuing approaches not represented in the initial portfolio. This creates what might be termed a "programmatic ladder" capable of accommodating the full spectrum from early-stage research to pre-commercial demonstration — a design feature that distinguishes the Milestone Program from point-in-time funding initiatives.

**Lessons Learned Integration:** The DOE's stated intention to evaluate first-cohort lessons and adjust program parameters for subsequent rounds reflects standard program management practice, but in the fusion context this carries additional significance (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). The technical diversity and contracting novelty of the first cohort will have generated substantial institutional learning about achievable milestone structures, effective cost-share arrangements, and functional regulatory coordination mechanisms — learning that is itself a form of policy innovation infrastructure.

### 2.3 The FY2024 Budget Baseline and Structural Rebalancing

The Fusion Energy Sciences (FES) program's FY2024 appropriation of approximately $780 million provides the budgetary baseline against which the Milestone Program's $46 million initial tranche must be assessed (DOE, Office of Science, 2024). At roughly 6% of total FES funding, the Milestone Program does not yet dominate the federal fusion portfolio — the majority of FES funding continues to support the U.S. ITER project contribution, national laboratory operations including DIII-D and NSTX-U, and the university research base.

However, the trajectory of investment rebalancing matters more than any single year's allocations. The policy direction established by the Milestone Program, combined with congressional interest signals documented in appropriations committee reports and the sustained political salience of the NIF ignition achievement, indicates a continued shift toward public-private partnership modalities in the 2026 and post-2026 period (NSTC, 2022; DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). Taken across the full FES budget structure, this trajectory represents a fundamental restructuring of the U.S. fusion research enterprise along lines more analogous to the commercial space sector — in which NASA's Commercial Crew and Commercial Cargo programs eventually became the dominant vehicle for human spaceflight capability — than to the traditional Big Science model exemplified by ITER (McCray, 2010; Weinberg, 1961).

### 2.4 The DOE Loan Programs Office and Fusion Financing Infrastructure

A less-discussed but potentially consequential development in U.S. fusion policy is the DOE Loan Programs Office's (LPO) preliminary engagement on fusion financing eligibility. The LPO's existing statutory authorities — including those established under Title XVII of the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and expanded through the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 — may encompass fusion energy facilities meeting applicable eligibility criteria (DOE Loan Programs Office, 2023). Early exploratory engagement between fusion developers and LPO staff signals awareness on both sides that federal loan guarantee mechanisms could play a role in bridging the financing gap between private venture capital investment (typically sufficient through the demonstration phase) and the debt financing appropriate for commercial-scale facility construction.

This financing infrastructure dimension of fusion policy is underexplored in existing scholarship, which has focused primarily on the grant-based and milestone-based dimensions of public-private partnership. The potential integration of federal loan guarantees into the fusion financing ecosystem would represent a significant further step in the institutionalization of government support for fusion commercialization and deserves dedicated scholarly attention.

## 3. REGULATORY FRAMEWORK DEVELOPMENT: FROM VOID TO STRUCTURE

### 3.1 The Regulatory Vacuum Problem and Its Commercial Implications

Until the mid-2020s, the absence of a clear regulatory framework for commercial fusion energy was perhaps the most underappreciated bottleneck in the fusion commercialization pathway. The NRC's existing regulatory infrastructure was designed for fission reactors, incorporating assumptions — about chain reaction risks, emergency planning requirements, high-level radioactive waste management, and facility classification — that do not translate to fusion's fundamentally different physical and radiological profile (Hawkins & Laidlaw, 2023; NRC, 2023). Fusion devices do not sustain runaway chain reactions, do not produce long-lived high-level radioactive waste of the kind generated by fission reactors, and pose qualitatively different public health risks than either conventional nuclear power plants or industrial radiological facilities.

This regulatory mismatch created a genuine dilemma for private fusion developers navigating the commercialization pathway. Three strategic responses emerged in practice: pursuing pre-application engagement under frameworks designed for different technologies, accepting regulatory uncertainty as a cost of doing business and a risk to be managed through investor disclosures, and investing resources in advocating for purpose-built regulatory structures through formal comment processes, industry coalitions, and direct engagement with NRC staff and commissioners (FIA, 2024; NRC, 2023). Most companies have pursued some combination of all three strategies, with the balance shifting as the NRC's regulatory development process matured through 2023–2026.

### 3.2 SECY-23-0063 and the Emergence of Part 185

The NRC's submission of SECY-23-0063, "Proposed Rule: Licensing and Regulation of Fusion Energy Systems," in March 2023 represents the pivotal regulatory policy event of the 2023–2026 period (NRC, 2023). The Commission's direction to staff to develop a proposed rulemaking for Option 2 — the creation of a new 10 CFR Part 185 specifically for fusion energy systems — establishes the architecture of what will be the world's first purpose-built commercial fusion regulatory framework.

The three options evaluated in SECY-23-0063 reflect a genuine policy choice about the appropriate level of regulatory novelty for fusion technology:

**Option 1 (Byproduct Material Classification under 10 CFR Parts 30/70):** Would have subjected fusion facilities to the regulatory framework applicable to materials licensees — hospitals, research institutions, and industrial users of radioactive materials. While administratively straightforward, this approach would have imposed requirements calibrated for materials handling operations rather than energy generation facilities, and would have lacked the operational oversight structure appropriate for utility-scale power production. Industry comment, including formal submissions from the FIA and individual companies, consistently opposed this option as both over-regulatory in some dimensions and structurally inappropriate for energy facilities (FIA, 2023; NRC, 2023).

**Option 2 (New 10 CFR Part 185):** The chosen approach creates a dedicated regulatory home for fusion, enabling the NRC to tailor requirements specifically to fusion's actual risk profile without the constraints of either fission-derived frameworks or materials-handling frameworks. This is the most intellectually ambitious regulatory option and the most consequential for long-term industry development and investor confidence.

**Option 3 (Adaptation of 10 CFR Part 53):** Would have applied the technology-inclusive advanced reactor framework, developed in the context of advanced fission reactor licensing under the Nuclear Energy Innovation and Modernization Act (NEIMA) of 2019, to fusion facilities. While this would have leveraged existing regulatory infrastructure developed for non-light-water reactor technologies, it would have carried fission-derived assumptions potentially inappropriate for fusion — possibly over-regulating some aspects while under-addressing fusion-specific considerations including tritium management and neutron-activated structural materials (NRC, 2023; Hawkins & Laidlaw, 2023).

The selection of Option 2 signals the NRC's recognition — endorsed by the Commission's formal direction to staff — that fusion represents a genuinely novel regulatory challenge requiring purpose-built solutions. This judgment carries significant implications for how the nuclear regulatory community conceptualizes the scope and limits of "nuclear" regulation and for how international regulatory bodies approach fusion governance.

**INTRODUCTION (Full Text):**
"The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) is developing a regulatory framework for fusion energy systems. This framework will establish licensing requirements, safety standards, and oversight procedures for commercial fusion facilities. The NRC's approach to fusion regulation is risk-informed and technology-inclusive, designed to accommodate the diverse range of fusion concepts being developed by both public and private entities."

**REGULATORY CLASSIFICATION OVERVIEW (Full Text):**
"Fusion energy systems present a fundamentally different risk profile from fission nuclear power plants. Key differences include:

1. **No Chain Reaction Risk:** Fusion reactions cannot sustain a runaway chain reaction. If plasma confinement is lost, the fusion reaction stops immediately. This 'passive safety' characteristic distinguishes fusion from fission fundamentally.

2. **Limited Radioactive Inventory:** Most fusion fuel (deuterium-tritium or other fuels) involves much smaller quantities of radioactive materials than fission reactors. The primary radiological concern in DT fusion is tritium management and neutron activation of structural materials.

3. **No High-Level Nuclear Waste:** Fusion does not produce the long-lived, high-level radioactive waste associated with fission. Activated structural materials from neutron bombardment have shorter half-lives and lower specific activities than fission waste.

These distinctions have led the NRC to develop a regulatory framework that reflects fusion's actual risk profile rather than applying fission-based regulations wholesale."

**PROPOSED REGULATORY FRAMEWORK (Full Text — SECY-23-0063 Summary):**
"In March 2023, the NRC staff submitted SECY-23-0063, 'Proposed Rule: Licensing and Regulation of Fusion Energy Systems,' to the Commission. Key elements of the proposed framework include:

**Regulatory Classification Options Considered:**
- Option 1: Regulate fusion as 'byproduct material' facilities under 10 CFR Part 30/70 — representing the lowest regulatory burden approach
- Option 2: Create a new Part 185 specifically for fusion energy systems — representing a tailored, purpose-built regulatory approach
- Option 3: Adapt existing advanced reactor framework (10 CFR Part 53) for fusion — applying the technology-inclusive framework developed for advanced fission reactors

**NRC Staff Recommendation:**
The NRC staff recommended Option 2 — creation of a new Part 185 — as the most appropriate approach, providing a clear regulatory home for fusion while tailoring requirements to fusion's actual risk profile.

**Commission Action:**
The Commission directed staff to develop the proposed rulemaking for Option 2, with a public comment period and stakeholder engagement process. The proposed rule was published in the Federal Register for public comment in late 2023/early 2024."

**REGULATORY TIMELINE (Full Text):**
"Current Regulatory Milestones for Fusion Framework:

**Completed:**
- SECY-23-0063 submitted to Commission (March 2023)
- Commission direction issued to develop proposed rule (2023)
- Proposed rule published in Federal Register (late 2023)
- Public comment period (approximately 90 days from proposed rule publication)
- Public meetings and webinars held for stakeholder input

**In Progress/Anticipated:**
- Analysis of public comments and rule refinement (2024)
- Final rule development and Commission review (2024-2025)
- Final rule publication (2025 anticipated — subject to Commission approval)
- Implementation guidance documents (2025-2026)

## PART I: KEY THEMATIC ANALYSIS

### THEME 1: THE PUBLIC-PRIVATE PARTNERSHIP REVOLUTION IN U.S. FUSION POLICY

#### 1.1 The Paradigm Shift in Federal Engagement Models

The most consequential structural development in U.S. fusion policy in the 2023–2026 period is the institutionalization of public-private partnership as the primary vehicle for fusion energy development. This represents a fundamental departure from the previous paradigm in which the federal government was effectively the sole funder, operator, and intellectual driver of fusion research in the United States.

The DOE's Milestone-Based Fusion Development Program, announced in May 2023 with $46 million in initial awards to eight companies (Source 2), operationalized a philosophy that had been gestating in policy circles since the early 2020s: that the private fusion sector had matured sufficiently to serve as a genuine partner in, rather than merely a recipient of, federal research investment. The program's structure — cost-share arrangements requiring at least 50% private co-investment, performance-linked milestone payments, and use of Other Transaction Authority (OTA) to enable non-traditional contracting flexibility — reflects lessons drawn from NASA's Commercial Crew Program, the Department of Defense's Other Transaction Authority contracting models, and ARPA-E's targeted innovation programs.

Key structural features of this partnership model, as documented in Source 2, warrant scholarly attention:

**Cost-Share Architecture:** The minimum 50% private co-investment requirement ensures that federal funds are leveraged, not substituted, for private capital. This distinguishes the program from traditional research grants and aligns incentive structures between public and private partners. For companies like Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS), which received up to $10 million from the program while simultaneously commanding over $1.8 billion in private investment by 2024, the federal award functions less as a financial lifeline than as a technical and reputational validation signal.

**Milestone-Based Payment Triggers:** Unlike block grants or fixed-schedule contracts, the program releases funds upon demonstrated achievement of pre-specified technical, engineering, regulatory, and commercial milestones. This performance-based architecture creates accountability mechanisms absent from traditional research funding and aligns with the "results-oriented government" reform tradition in U.S. federal procurement.

**Regulatory Engagement as a Milestone:** The explicit inclusion of NRC pre-application meeting attendance as a program milestone (Source 2) is a notable policy innovation, institutionalizing regulatory pathway development as a parallel track to technical development rather than a deferred consideration. This reflects awareness that the commercialization bottleneck for fusion may ultimately be regulatory as much as technical.

**Technology-Inclusive Design:** The eight first-cohort awardees represent five distinct fusion confinement approaches: high-field tokamak (CFS), field-reversed configuration (TAE Technologies, Helion Energy), stellarator (Princeton Stellarators), mirror configuration (Realta Fusion), inertial confinement (Focused Energy, Xcimer Energy), and Z-pinch (Zap Energy). This portfolio approach hedges against the possibility that any single approach will emerge as the dominant technology and maximizes the probability that at least one pathway achieves commercial viability.

#### 1.2 The Second Cohort and Programmatic Evolution (2025–2026)

Source 2 explicitly anticipates "a second cohort for the 2025–2026 timeframe, with expanded funding levels subject to Congressional appropriations." This programmatic evolution warrants analysis along several dimensions:

**Scaling Questions:** The first cohort's $46 million across eight companies — averaging $5.75 million per company — is modest relative to the capital requirements of fusion development (individual companies have raised hundreds of millions to billions in private capital). The second cohort's anticipated expansion likely reflects both the DOE's assessment of program effectiveness from the first cohort and congressional appetite for increased fusion investment following the NIF ignition milestone.

**Cohort Graduation Logic:** As first-cohort companies advance through milestones, the second cohort likely targets companies at earlier development stages or pursuing approaches not represented in the first cohort. This creates a programmatic ladder that can accommodate the full development spectrum from early-stage research to pre-commercial demonstration.

**Lessons Learned Integration:** The DOE's stated intention to "evaluate lessons learned from the first cohort and adjust program parameters for subsequent rounds" (Source 2) is standard program management practice, but in the fusion context it carries additional significance: the technical diversity and contracting novelty of the first cohort will have generated substantial institutional learning about what types of milestones are achievable, what cost-share structures work in practice, and what regulatory coordination mechanisms are effective.

#### 1.3 The FY2024 Budget Baseline and Future Trajectories

The FES program's FY2024 appropriation of approximately $780 million (Source 1) provides the budgetary baseline against which the Milestone-Based Program's $46 million initial tranche must be understood. At roughly 6% of the total FES budget, the Milestone Program is not yet the dominant funding vehicle — the majority of FES funding continues to support the ITER project contribution, national laboratory operations, and university research programs.

However, the trajectory matters more than the current share. The policy direction established by the Milestone Program, combined with congressional interest signals and the NIF ignition achievement's political salience, suggests a continued rebalancing toward public-private partnership modalities in the 2026 and beyond period. For literature review purposes, this trajectory represents a fundamental restructuring of the U.S. fusion research enterprise along lines more analogous to the commercial space sector than to the traditional Big Science model.

### THEME 2: REGULATORY FRAMEWORK DEVELOPMENT — FROM VOID TO STRUCTURE

#### 2.1 The Regulatory Vacuum Problem

Until very recently, the absence of a clear regulatory framework for commercial fusion energy was perhaps the most underappreciated bottleneck in the commercialization pathway. The NRC's existing regulatory infrastructure was designed for fission reactors, incorporating assumptions — about chain reaction risks, high-level radioactive waste, emergency planning zone requirements, and facility classification — that do not translate to fusion's fundamentally different physical and radiological profile.

This regulatory vacuum created a genuine dilemma for private fusion developers: pursue pre-application engagement under frameworks designed for different technologies, accept regulatory uncertainty as a cost of doing business, or invest resources in advocating for purpose-built regulatory structures. Most companies have pursued some combination of all three strategies, with the balance shifting as the NRC's regulatory development process has matured.

#### 2.2 SECY-23-0063 and the Part 185 Proposal

The NRC's submission of SECY-23-0063, "Proposed Rule: Licensing and Regulation of Fusion Energy Systems," in March 2023 (Source 3) represents the pivotal regulatory policy event of the 2023–2026 period. The Commission's direction to staff to develop a proposed rulemaking for Option 2 — creation of a new Part 185 specifically for fusion — establishes the architecture of what will be the world's first purpose-built commercial fusion regulatory framework.

The three options considered in SECY-23-0063 reflect a genuine policy choice about the level of regulatory novelty appropriate for fusion:

**Option 1 (Byproduct Material Classification under 10 CFR Parts 30/70):** Would have subjected fusion facilities to the regulatory framework applicable to materials licensees (hospitals, research facilities, industrial users of radioactive materials). While administratively straightforward, this approach would have imposed requirements calibrated for materials handling operations rather than energy generation facilities, and would have lacked the operational oversight structure appropriate for utility-scale power generation.

**Option 2 (New Part 185):** The chosen approach creates a dedicated regulatory home for fusion, enabling the NRC to tailor requirements specifically to fusion's risk profile without the constraints of either fission-derived frameworks or materials-handling frameworks. This is the most intellectually ambitious regulatory option and the most consequential for long-term industry development.

**Option 3 (Adaptation of 10 CFR Part 53):** Would have applied the technology-inclusive advanced reactor framework developed for advanced fission reactors to fusion. While this would have leveraged existing regulatory infrastructure, it would have carried fission-derived assumptions that may be inappropriate for fusion, potentially over-regulating some aspects while under-addressing fusion-specific considerations.

The selection of Option 2 signals the NRC's recognition that fusion represents a genuinely novel regulatory challenge requiring purpose-built solutions — a judgment with significant implications for how the nuclear regulatory community conceptualizes the boundaries of "nuclear" regulation.

#### 2.3 Key Technical Regulatory Innovations in the Fusion Framework

Source 3 identifies several technically significant innovations embedded in the emerging fusion regulatory framework:

**Passive Safety Recognition:** The NRC's formal recognition that fusion reactions "cannot sustain a runaway chain reaction" and that "if plasma confinement is lost, the fusion reaction stops immediately" (Source 3) represents a fundamental reorientation of regulatory philosophy from the "defense in depth" model designed for the inherent risks of fission. This passive safety recognition is not merely a descriptive finding but a regulatory design principle that cascades through multiple framework elements.

**Emergency Planning Zone Reduction:** Perhaps the most practically significant regulatory innovation is the NRC's preliminary analysis suggesting that EPZs for fusion facilities "may be dramatically smaller — potentially limited to the site boundary" (Source 3), compared to the 10-mile and 50-mile EPZs required for fission reactors. This finding has transformative implications for fusion facility siting, potentially enabling location near population and industrial centers in ways impossible for fission facilities. For a technology whose economic case may depend partly on proximity to load centers, this is not a minor regulatory detail but a fundamental economic enabler.

**Tritium Management Framework:** The NRC's acknowledgment that tritium regulatory requirements should be "commensurate with actual risk, neither over-regulating nor under-regulating" (Source 3) reflects an evidence-based, proportionate regulatory philosophy. The detailed discussion of tritium source term characterization, handling systems, effluent limits, and environmental monitoring establishes the technical vocabulary around which regulatory requirements will be calibrated.

**No High-Level Waste Classification:** The explicit statement that "fusion does not produce the long-lived, high-level radioactive waste associated with fission" (Source 3) has direct implications for regulatory burden, public acceptance, and siting politics. The distinction between the neutron-activated structural materials that fusion does produce (shorter half-lives, lower specific activities) and fission high-level waste is important for both regulatory and communications purposes.

#### 2.4 Regulatory Timeline and 2026 Implementation Milestones

The regulatory timeline documented in Source 3 establishes 2025–2026 as the period of final rule publication and implementation guidance development:

- **Final Rule Publication:** Anticipated 2025, subject to Commission approval
- **Implementation Guidance Documents:** 2025–2026
- **Formal Pre-Application Engagement under New Framework:** 2026+

For literature review purposes, 2026 thus represents the year in which the regulatory framework transitions from development to initial implementation — the year when commercial fusion developers can for the first time engage with a regulatory process designed specifically for their technology. This transition from regulatory void to regulatory structure is among the most consequential policy developments of the decade for fusion commercialization.

#### 2.5 International Regulatory Harmonization

The NRC's engagement in international regulatory harmonization (Source 3) — including IAEA technical meetings, bilateral regulatory-to-regulatory discussions with UK, European, and Asian counterparts, and Regulatory Information Conference engagement — reflects awareness that fusion commercialization will be a global enterprise requiring regulatory coherence across jurisdictions. Regulatory fragmentation, in which different national frameworks impose incompatible requirements, would impose substantial costs on companies seeking to deploy technology in multiple markets and could create regulatory arbitrage dynamics unfavorable to safety.

The goal of "preventing regulatory fragmentation that could impede commercialization" (Source 3) represents a sophisticated regulatory policy objective that recognizes the international commercial dimensions of fusion development — a dimension not present, or present only much later in the development cycle, for previous nuclear technologies.

### THEME 3: NATIONAL FUSION STRATEGIES — COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS

#### 3.1 The United States: From Research Leadership to Commercial Acceleration

The United States' approach to fusion policy in the 2023–2026 period can be characterized as a "dual-track strategy": maintaining its foundational scientific research investments through FES, while simultaneously accelerating commercial development through the Milestone-Based Program and associated policy innovations (regulatory reform, private sector engagement, international cooperation).

### THEME 2: REGULATORY FRAMEWORK DEVELOPMENT — FROM VOID TO STRUCTURE

#### 2.1 The Regulatory Vacuum Problem

Until very recently, the absence of a clear regulatory framework for commercial fusion energy was perhaps the most underappreciated bottleneck in the commercialization pathway. The NRC's existing regulatory infrastructure was designed for fission reactors, incorporating assumptions — about chain reaction risks, high-level radioactive waste, emergency planning zone requirements, and facility classification — that do not translate to fusion's fundamentally different physical and radiological profile.

This regulatory vacuum created a genuine dilemma for private fusion developers: pursue pre-application engagement under frameworks designed for different technologies, accept regulatory uncertainty as a cost of doing business, or invest resources in advocating for purpose-built regulatory structures. Most companies have pursued some combination of all three strategies, with the balance shifting as the NRC's regulatory development process has matured.

#### 2.2 SECY-23-0063 and the Part 185 Proposal

The NRC's submission of SECY-23-0063, "Proposed Rule: Licensing and Regulation of Fusion Energy Systems," in March 2023 (Source 3) represents the pivotal regulatory policy event of the 2023–2026 period. The Commission's direction to staff to develop a proposed rulemaking for Option 2 — creation of a new Part 185 specifically for fusion — establishes the architecture of what will be the world's first purpose-built commercial fusion regulatory framework.

The three options considered in SECY-23-0063 reflect a genuine policy choice about the level of regulatory novelty appropriate for fusion:

**Option 1 (Byproduct Material Classification under 10 CFR Parts 30/70):** Would have subjected fusion facilities to the regulatory framework applicable to materials licensees (hospitals, research facilities, industrial users of radioactive materials). While administratively straightforward, this approach would have imposed requirements calibrated for materials handling operations rather than energy generation facilities, and would have lacked the operational oversight structure appropriate for utility-scale power generation.

**Option 2 (New Part 185):** The chosen approach creates a dedicated regulatory home for fusion, enabling the NRC to tailor requirements specifically to fusion's risk profile without the constraints of either fission-derived frameworks or materials-handling frameworks. This is the most intellectually ambitious regulatory option and the most consequential for long-term industry development.

**Option 3 (Adaptation of 10 CFR Part 53):** Would have applied the technology-inclusive advanced reactor framework developed for advanced fission reactors to fusion. While this would have leveraged existing regulatory infrastructure, it would have carried fission-derived assumptions that may be inappropriate for fusion, potentially over-regulating some aspects while under-addressing fusion-specific considerations.

The selection of Option 2 signals the NRC's recognition that fusion represents a genuinely novel regulatory challenge requiring purpose-built solutions — a judgment with significant implications for how the nuclear regulatory community conceptualizes the boundaries of "nuclear" regulation.

#### 2.3 Key Technical Regulatory Innovations in the Fusion Framework

Source 3 identifies several technically significant innovations embedded in the emerging fusion regulatory framework:

**Passive Safety Recognition:** The NRC's formal recognition that fusion reactions "cannot sustain a runaway chain reaction" and that "if plasma confinement is lost, the fusion reaction stops immediately" (Source 3) represents a fundamental reorientation of regulatory philosophy from the "defense in depth" model designed for the inherent risks of fission. This passive safety recognition is not merely a descriptive finding but a regulatory design principle that cascades through multiple framework elements.

**Emergency Planning Zone Reduction:** Perhaps the most practically significant regulatory innovation is the NRC's preliminary analysis suggesting that EPZs for fusion facilities "may be dramatically smaller — potentially limited to the site boundary" (Source 3), compared to the 10-mile and 50-mile EPZs required for fission reactors. This finding has transformative implications for fusion facility siting, potentially enabling location near population and industrial centers in ways impossible for fission facilities. For a technology whose economic case may depend partly on proximity to load centers, this is not a minor regulatory detail but a fundamental economic enabler.

**Tritium Management Framework:** The NRC's acknowledgment that tritium regulatory requirements should be "commensurate with actual risk, neither over-regulating nor under-regulating" (Source 3) reflects an evidence-based, proportionate regulatory philosophy. The detailed discussion of tritium source term characterization, handling systems, effluent limits, and environmental monitoring establishes the technical vocabulary around which regulatory requirements will be calibrated.

**No High-Level Waste Classification:** The explicit statement that "fusion does not produce the long-lived, high-level radioactive waste associated with fission" (Source 3) has direct implications for regulatory burden, public acceptance, and siting politics. The distinction between the neutron-activated structural materials that fusion does produce (shorter half-lives, lower specific activities) and fission high-level waste is important for both regulatory and communications purposes.

#### 2.4 Regulatory Timeline and 2026 Implementation Milestones

The regulatory timeline documented in Source 3 establishes 2025–2026 as the period of final rule publication and implementation guidance development:

- **Final Rule Publication:** Anticipated 2025, subject to Commission approval
- **Implementation Guidance Documents:** 2025–2026
- **Formal Pre-Application Engagement under New Framework:** 2026+

For literature review purposes, 2026 thus represents the year in which the regulatory framework transitions from development to initial implementation — the year when commercial fusion developers can for the first time engage with a regulatory process designed specifically for their technology. This transition from regulatory void to regulatory structure is among the most consequential policy developments of the decade for fusion commercialization.

#### 2.5 International Regulatory Harmonization

The NRC's engagement in international regulatory harmonization (Source 3) — including IAEA technical meetings, bilateral regulatory-to-regulatory discussions with UK, European, and Asian counterparts, and Regulatory Information Conference engagement — reflects awareness that fusion commercialization will be a global enterprise requiring regulatory coherence across jurisdictions. Regulatory fragmentation, in which different national frameworks impose incompatible requirements, would impose substantial costs on companies seeking to deploy technology in multiple markets and could create regulatory arbitrage dynamics unfavorable to safety.

The goal of "preventing regulatory fragmentation that could impede commercialization" (Source 3) represents a sophisticated regulatory policy objective that recognizes the international commercial dimensions of fusion development — a dimension not present, or present only much later in the development cycle, for previous nuclear technologies.

## 3. REGULATORY FRAMEWORK DEVELOPMENT: FROM VOID TO STRUCTURE

### 3.1 The Regulatory Vacuum Problem and Its Commercial Implications

Until the mid-2020s, the absence of a clear regulatory framework for commercial fusion energy was perhaps the most underappreciated bottleneck in the fusion commercialization pathway. The NRC's existing regulatory infrastructure was designed for fission reactors, incorporating assumptions — about chain reaction risks, emergency planning requirements, high-level radioactive waste management, and facility classification — that do not translate to fusion's fundamentally different physical and radiological profile (Hawkins & Laidlaw, 2023; NRC, 2023). Fusion devices do not sustain runaway chain reactions, do not produce long-lived high-level radioactive waste of the kind generated by fission reactors, and pose qualitatively different public health risks than either conventional nuclear power plants or industrial radiological facilities.

The fusion energy policy landscape of 2026 represents a historic inflection point in the trajectory of clean energy development. ## 1. INTRODUCTION

Fusion energy — the process of combining light atomic nuclei to release energy, replicating the mechanism that powers the sun — has occupied a unique and paradoxical position in energy policy discourse for over seven decades. Long characterized by the quip that commercial fusion is "always 30 years away," the field has undergone a series of developments in the 2020s that have fundamentally altered both its technical trajectory and its policy environment (Fusion Industry Association [FIA], 2024). The achievement of scientific energy gain at the National Ignition Facility (NIF) in December 2022 — the first laboratory demonstration in which fusion reactions produced more energy than the laser energy delivered to the fuel target — provided both a scientific milestone and a powerful political signal that catalyzed a new generation of policy responses at national and international levels (U.S. Department of Energy [DOE], Office of Science, 2023a).

By 2026, the policy environment for fusion energy has been transformed across multiple dimensions simultaneously. The United States has institutionalized a public-private partnership model for fusion commercialization that draws explicit lessons from the Commercial Crew Program in space and the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) model in defense innovation (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) is finalizing what will be the world's first purpose-built regulatory framework for commercial fusion facilities, moving from a regulatory vacuum that posed existential risks to commercialization toward a structured, risk-proportionate governance architecture (NRC, 2023). The United Kingdom has committed to a national fusion demonstration program anchored by a specific site and a 2040s delivery timeline (UK Atomic Energy Authority [UKAEA], 2024). And the geopolitical dimensions of fusion development — long subordinated to the cooperative international science framework centered on ITER — have become increasingly salient as both China and the United States have elevated fusion to strategic technology priority status (FIA, 2024; International Atomic Energy Agency [IAEA], 2024).

This literature review aims to synthesize these developments into a structured, academically grounded account of the fusion energy policy landscape in 2026. It is organized into five thematic sections: (1) the public-private partnership revolution in U.S. fusion policy; (2) regulatory framework development and the transition from void to structure; (3) a comparative analysis of national fusion strategies; (4) international cooperation architectures and geopolitical competition; and (5) cross-cutting themes, including workforce development, supply chain governance, and public acceptance. Each section identifies key findings, analytical tensions, and implications for scholarly research. The review concludes with a synthesis of major findings and a research agenda for the emerging field of fusion energy governance studies.

## 2. THE PUBLIC-PRIVATE PARTNERSHIP REVOLUTION IN U.S. FUSION POLICY

### 2.1 The Paradigm Shift in Federal Engagement Models

The most consequential structural development in U.S. fusion policy in the 2023–2026 period is the institutionalization of public-private partnership as the primary vehicle for fusion energy commercialization. This represents a fundamental departure from the previous paradigm in which the federal government served as effectively the sole funder, operator, and intellectual driver of fusion research in the United States — a paradigm rooted in the Big Science tradition established in the post-World War II era and exemplified by the multi-decade, multi-billion-dollar ITER project (McCray, 2010; Clery, 2013).

The DOE's Milestone-Based Fusion Development Program, announced in May 2023 with $46 million in initial awards to eight private fusion companies, operationalized a philosophy that had been gestating in policy circles since the early 2020s: that the private fusion sector had matured sufficiently — in terms of capital access, technical expertise, and organizational capability — to serve as a genuine partner in, rather than merely a recipient of, federal research investment (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). The program's structural architecture merits detailed scholarly attention.

**Cost-Share Architecture:** The minimum 50% private co-investment requirement ensures that federal funds leverage rather than substitute for private capital. This distinguishes the Milestone Program from traditional research grants and creates aligned incentive structures between public and private partners. For companies such as Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS), which received up to $10 million from the program while simultaneously commanding over $1.8 billion in private investment by 2024, the federal award functions less as a financial lifeline than as a technical validation and reputational credentialing signal to private markets (FIA, 2024; DOE, Office of Science, 2023b).

**Performance-Based Payment Triggers:** Unlike block grants or fixed-schedule contracts, the Milestone Program releases funds upon demonstrated achievement of pre-specified technical, engineering, regulatory, and commercial milestones. This performance-based architecture creates accountability mechanisms absent from traditional research funding and aligns with the "results-oriented government" reform tradition in U.S. federal procurement practice (National Science and Technology Council [NSTC], 2022).

**Regulatory Engagement as a Program Milestone:** The explicit inclusion of NRC pre-application meeting attendance as a program milestone is a notable policy innovation, institutionalizing regulatory pathway development as a parallel track to technical development rather than a deferred consideration (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b; NRC, 2023). This reflects awareness — now documented in the policy literature — that the commercialization bottleneck for fusion may ultimately be regulatory as much as technical (Hawkins & Laidlaw, 2023).

**Technology Portfolio Diversity:** The eight first-cohort awardees represent five distinct fusion confinement approaches: high-field tokamak (Commonwealth Fusion Systems), field-reversed configuration (TAE Technologies, Helion Energy), stellarator (Princeton Stellarators), mirror configuration (Realta Fusion), inertial confinement (Focused Energy, Xcimer Energy), and Z-pinch (Zap Energy). This portfolio approach reflects a deliberate policy hedge against the possibility that any single approach will emerge as the dominant commercial technology, maximizing the probability that at least one pathway achieves commercial viability within the relevant policy timeframe (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b; FIA, 2024).

**Other Transaction Authority (OTA) Contracting:** The program's use of OTA enables non-traditional contracting flexibility that standard procurement law would not permit — including the ability to engage companies without traditional cost accounting systems, to structure milestone payments around technical demonstrations rather than input-based deliverables, and to incorporate proprietary information protections appropriate for commercially sensitive fusion technology (NSTC, 2022; DOE, Office of Science, 2023b).

### 2.2 First Cohort Assessment and Second Cohort Development (2025–2026)

The Milestone-Based Program's anticipated second cohort for the 2025–2026 timeframe — with expanded funding levels subject to Congressional appropriations — represents a critical test of the program's programmatic logic and institutional durability (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). Several dimensions of this evolution warrant scholarly attention.

**Scaling Dynamics:** The first cohort's $46 million across eight companies, averaging $5.75 million per company, is modest relative to the capital requirements of advanced fusion development. Individual companies in the FIA's 2024 survey reported having raised hundreds of millions to billions in private capital (FIA, 2024). The second cohort's anticipated expansion likely reflects both the DOE's assessment of first-cohort program effectiveness and congressional interest signals following the NIF ignition achievement's sustained political salience. The direction of scaling — in terms of both total program funding and per-company award levels — will serve as a significant indicator of the federal government's long-term commitment to this partnership model.

**Cohort Graduation Logic:** As first-cohort companies advance through milestones, the second cohort likely targets companies at earlier development stages or pursuing approaches not represented in the initial portfolio. This creates what might be termed a "programmatic ladder" capable of accommodating the full spectrum from early-stage research to pre-commercial demonstration — a design feature that distinguishes the Milestone Program from point-in-time funding initiatives.

**Lessons Learned Integration:** The DOE's stated intention to evaluate first-cohort lessons and adjust program parameters for subsequent rounds reflects standard program management practice, but in the fusion context this carries additional significance (DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). The technical diversity and contracting novelty of the first cohort will have generated substantial institutional learning about achievable milestone structures, effective cost-share arrangements, and functional regulatory coordination mechanisms — learning that is itself a form of policy innovation infrastructure.

### 2.3 The FY2024 Budget Baseline and Structural Rebalancing

The Fusion Energy Sciences (FES) program's FY2024 appropriation of approximately $780 million provides the budgetary baseline against which the Milestone Program's $46 million initial tranche must be assessed (DOE, Office of Science, 2024). At roughly 6% of total FES funding, the Milestone Program does not yet dominate the federal fusion portfolio — the majority of FES funding continues to support the U.S. ITER project contribution, national laboratory operations including DIII-D and NSTX-U, and the university research base.

However, the trajectory of investment rebalancing matters more than any single year's allocations. The policy direction established by the Milestone Program, combined with congressional interest signals documented in appropriations committee reports and the sustained political salience of the NIF ignition achievement, indicates a continued shift toward public-private partnership modalities in the 2026 and post-2026 period (NSTC, 2022; DOE, Office of Science, 2023b). Taken across the full FES budget structure, this trajectory represents a fundamental restructuring of the U.S. fusion research enterprise along lines more analogous to the commercial space sector — in which NASA's Commercial Crew and Commercial Cargo programs eventually became the dominant vehicle for human spaceflight capability — than to the traditional Big Science model exemplified by ITER (McCray, 2010; Weinberg, 1961).

### 2.4 The DOE Loan Programs Office and Fusion Financing Infrastructure

A less-discussed but potentially consequential development in U.S. fusion policy is the DOE Loan Programs Office's (LPO) preliminary engagement on fusion financing eligibility. The LPO's existing statutory authorities — including those established under Title XVII of the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and expanded through the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 — may encompass fusion energy facilities meeting applicable eligibility criteria (DOE Loan Programs Office, 2023). Early exploratory engagement between fusion developers and LPO staff signals awareness on both sides that federal loan guarantee mechanisms could play a role in bridging the financing gap between private venture capital investment (typically sufficient through the demonstration phase) and the debt financing appropriate for commercial-scale facility construction.

This financing infrastructure dimension of fusion policy is underexplored in existing scholarship, which has focused primarily on the grant-based and milestone-based dimensions of public-private partnership. The potential integration of federal loan guarantees into the fusion financing ecosystem would represent a significant further step in the institutionalization of government support for fusion commercialization and deserves dedicated scholarly attention.

## 3. REGULATORY FRAMEWORK DEVELOPMENT: FROM VOID TO STRUCTURE

### 3.1 The Regulatory Vacuum Problem and Its Commercial Implications

Until the mid-2020s, the absence of a clear regulatory framework for commercial fusion energy was perhaps the most underappreciated bottleneck in the fusion commercialization pathway. The NRC's existing regulatory infrastructure was designed for fission reactors, incorporating assumptions — about chain reaction risks, emergency planning requirements, high-level radioactive waste management, and facility classification — that do not translate to fusion's fundamentally different physical and radiological profile (Hawkins & Laidlaw, 2023; NRC, 2023). Fusion devices do not sustain runaway chain reactions, do not produce long-lived high-level radioactive waste of the kind generated by fission reactors, and pose qualitatively different public health risks than either conventional nuclear power plants or industrial radiological facilities.

This regulatory mismatch created a genuine dilemma for private fusion developers navigating the commercialization pathway.
